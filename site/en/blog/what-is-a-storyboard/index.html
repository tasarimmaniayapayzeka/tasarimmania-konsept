What Is a Storyboard For, and What Does It Save Before the Shoot?

Most teams take this step for decoration and skip it at the first opportunity. But the point of drawing isn't to produce a handsome document; it's to close decisions before the shoot. A frame rubbed out on paper costs nothing; the same frame rubbed out on set costs a waiting crew an hour. In this piece we cover what it does, how long it takes, and when you need to move on to an animatic.

Why Does Making a Storyboard Come Before the Shoot?

Because the cheapest place to argue about a frame is at the desk. At the storyboard stage, changing a framing takes minutes; on set you make the same change while the crew waits. A shot rubbed out on paper wins an hour back on the shooting day. Preparation isn't a cost; it's the line item with the highest return. We measure that again on every project.

On set everyone waits at once: the crew, the equipment, the cast and the location. That's why every decision made there gets more expensive by a multiplier. Making the same decision at a desk in pre-production spends only a few people's time.

We set out how the stages run in order in the production stages piece. In the preparation round, the storyboard is the most visible output of that order.

You see the return on this step on the shooting day. If the storyboard round is complete, the set focuses only on execution; if it isn't, you ask the same questions while the crew waits. Going through the storyboard together while it's being made is the step that creates that difference.

Is It Needed on Small Jobs Too?

It may not be for a single-shot job. But once there is more than one shot, the argument about sequence begins, and that argument doesn't run without paper. On short jobs this preparation takes half an hour; half an hour is all you gain by skipping it.

What Does a Storyboard Show, and What Doesn't It?

It shows framing, scale and sequence. Performance, the quality of the light and rhythm don't appear on paper. That's why making a storyboard isn't an art exercise but a decision document. The beauty of the drawing doesn't matter; being understood does. A frame drawn with stick figures beats a detailed but ambiguous one.

We put two lines of notes beside every frame: what the camera is doing and what is happening in the scene. Those notes do more work than the drawing, because paper doesn't carry movement.

There are four pieces of information a frame has to carry.

  1. Framing — what the camera sees, where the product sits.
  2. Scale — is it a wide shot or a close-up.
  3. Movement — is the camera or the subject moving.
  4. Sequence number — where this frame sits in which shot.

The framing decisions are settled here. Where in frame will the product sit, will the face or the product come forward, where will the empty space fall? It also helps to think about composition principles alongside the hierarchy logic in the interface design principles piece. Do You Need to Be Able to Draw?

No. Boxes, arrows and stick figures do the job. The aim is to convey who is standing where and what the camera sees; that needs clarity, not talent.

How Many Frames Do You Need to Draw?

At least one frame per shot. If there's movement, we draw the opening and closing frames separately. Fifteen to twenty-five frames is normal on a thirty-second film. The time making a storyboard takes grows with the number of decisions, not the number of frames. Drawing the same frame three times takes longer than drawing three different ones.

The story sets the number of frames, not the duration. A thirty-second film moving on fast cuts wants far more frames than a single-shot minute.

A crowded frame list is a warning in itself. If you're trying to fit forty frames into thirty seconds, the problem isn't in the drawing but in the script.

Don't forget to number the frames either. On set, the shot list and the frames are matched by number; an unnumbered draft means minutes spent searching. That is the dullest but most useful detail of making a storyboard.

How Many Rounds Does Approval Take?

Two rounds is normal. If a third round is going round, the issue isn't the framing but the thing you're trying to say. At that point we take a step back and talk the script through again.

Which Mistakes Does a Storyboard Catch Early?

It catches three mistakes regularly. The product falls out of frame, the sequence breaks the story, or the aspect ratio doesn't fit. If you're publishing vertically, you have to see that on paper. A ratio problem you notice during the storyboard round closes far more cheaply than one noticed after the shoot. These three are the most common.

The ratio question is the most insidious. When a shot filmed horizontally is cropped to vertical, the product usually ends up outside the frame; you only notice on publication day. Checking the frame draft separately for each ratio removes that risk entirely.

The sequence mistake closes just as cheaply. Laying the frames out on the desk and moving them around takes minutes; the same experiment with filmed footage costs an edit round. We covered how losses like these land on the budget in the cost line items piece.

A framing mistake closes cheaply on the same logic. Marking on paper whether the product's label is legible takes seconds. Notice the same problem after the shoot and you're left with two roads: a reshoot, or a crop in the edit. Neither is one you want. A minute on paper is an hour on set.

What Is an Animatic, and When Do You Need One?

A moving draft is the frames spread out over time. You line the frames up and watch them with music; that way you hear the rhythm before the shoot. It earns its keep on complex flows and long films. Once making the storyboard is finished, moving to an animatic takes a few hours, but it closes the argument about tempo entirely. On short, simple jobs you don't need one.

Rhythm doesn't show on paper. A run of five frames can be too fast or too slow; you only find out which by spreading it over time. The animatic closes that uncertainty.

Watching it with music is a separate gain. You see early where the cut points fall against the beats of the track. That alone dissolves a round at the edit stage.

Who Makes the Animatic?

The edit team makes it, and it usually takes a few hours. Since the frames are already in hand, the job amounts to spreading them over time and dropping in a temporary track. While it's being made, the gaps left in the storyboard show up of their own accord. Don't move on to an animatic before the storyboard round is complete.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions: making a storyboard

How long does making a storyboard take?

It varies with scope, but a few days is enough for most jobs. On a thirty-second film set in one location, the frames come out in a day; with the approval rounds, that stretches to two or three. On jobs with a large cast, several locations or animation, it widens towards a week. What lengthens the time isn't the drawing but the number of decisions; if the script is clear, the frames come quickly. Settling who has authority to approve, right at the start, also shortens this noticeably.

I can't draw. Can I still do it?

Boxes and stick figures are enough.

Can I use reference images instead of a storyboard?

Partly. References are good at conveying tone and light, but they don't show the framing of your scene. Using both together is the soundest route.

Should I draw separate frames for the vertical and horizontal versions?

Check the same frame in both ratios. Separate drawings aren't essential, but you do need to see that the product stays in frame in both.

Who approves the frames?

One person with the authority to decide. When approval is scattered, the rounds multiply and the schedule slips.

Can we depart from the frames on set?

You can, and usually you do. The frames aren't a contract but common ground; as long as the departure is deliberate, it causes no trouble.

Is it needed on animation jobs too?

There it matters even more. Because every frame in animation carries a production cost, any decision not made at the drawing stage costs many times more later.

What tool is it made with?

Pencil and paper are enough for most jobs.
