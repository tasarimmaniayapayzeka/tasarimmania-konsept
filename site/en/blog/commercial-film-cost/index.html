What Line Items Set the Price of a Commercial Film?

The question in the first meeting is always the same: what will this film cost? The honest answer is that no figure can be given until the scope is down in writing. There's no price list below; there are the line items that move the budget. Once you know them, you can read for yourself where the difference between two quotes comes from.

Which Line Items Set the Price of a Commercial Film?

Six do: the shooting day, the crew, the cast, the location, the equipment and post-production. Any figure given before those six are settled is a guess. What really grows the price of a commercial film isn't the number of shots but how many separate setups those shots need. Ten shots in one location come cheaper than three shots across three.

When you read quotes, look for the line item names. The total is the least informative line on the page; it doesn't show what each item covers.

The six headings below come up on every project. Which one weighs heaviest depends on the kind of job.

  1. Number of shooting days — not how many shots, but how many separate setups.
  2. Crew size — how many people spend the day on set.
  3. Cast — not the fee, but the usage period and the breadth of channels.
  4. Location — rent, permits, closure and transport.
  5. Equipment — camera, lighting, movement systems.
  6. Post-production — colour, sound, music, subtitles, channel versions.

Why Isn't There a Ready-Made Price List?

Because very different jobs live under the same heading. A product-led promotional film shot in one location doesn't carry the same line items as a job with a cast across three locations. Whoever shares a ready-made list has narrowed the scope on your behalf; and the difference surfaces after the contract is signed.

How Does the Number of Shooting Days Affect the Budget?

The number of days creates a direct multiplier. The crew, the equipment hire and the location fee are all calculated by the day; the second day costs almost as much as the first. That's why we try to fit the shot list into a single day. The decision that brings the price of a commercial film down fastest is reducing the number of setups. Fewer setups means a shorter day, and a shorter day means a smaller budget.

Building a lighting setup takes hours. Five shots filmed under the same setup come far cheaper than five shots under five different ones. That's why we order the shot list by setup, not by the order of the story.

We set out how the stages work in the production stages piece. Knowing early in the pricing conversation how many setups the advertising idea calls for brings the estimate closer to reality.

Is There Such a Thing as a Half Day?

Equipment hire and crew day rates usually run on a full day. A half day only makes sense on very narrow jobs, and even then the preparation time stays the same.

Why Are the Cast and the Location So Decisive?

The cast fee isn't a one-off; the usage period and the breadth of channels enter the price. A year of digital use and unlimited television use don't come to the same figure. The gap is substantial. On the location side, permits, closure and transport are the invisible line items. Both should be written into the contract.

Two lines are what you look for in a performer's contract: on which channels, and for how long. If you want to republish the film two years later and that line isn't there, you're back at the negotiating table.

There's a similar situation on the location side. Closing a café for a shoot doesn't just mean rent; the trade the business loses that day enters the calculation too. Shooting in your own office removes that line item entirely.

The equipment line item swells with need as well. Camera and lighting are on every job. A dolly, a crane and a drone only go on the list if the shot calls for them. What really sets the price is which movement the advertising idea makes unavoidable, not the equipment itself.

An Agency Performer, or a Real Customer?

Filming with someone who genuinely uses your product can be both cheaper and more convincing. But being at ease in front of a camera is a skill, and not every real customer carries it.

How Does Post-Production Show Up in the Price?

The post-production line items are the ones most often skipped in a quote. Colour grade, voiceover, music licence and subtitles are each separate jobs. So is a separate version per channel. If animation or visual effects come in, the item grows fast. The price of a commercial film can quietly double in this section.

Music is the item that surprises people most often. There's a large gap between a track from a stock library and an original composition; which channels and which period the licence covers is a separate heading again.

Channel versions also take longer than people assume. The vertical, square and horizontal cuts of the same story are each edited separately; the six-second version comes out harder than the thirty-second one.

Subtitles make more work than they look like too. Every channel wants a different format; burnt-in subtitles and subtitles delivered as a separate file are two entirely different workflows. On a multilingual delivery, multiply the number of items by the number of languages. Music and voiceover are copyright matters; Türkiye's Directorate General of Copyright sets out how usage permission works.

Are Revision Rounds Included in the Price?

It should be written into the contract. Two rounds is normal; a promise of unlimited revisions is either built into the price from the start or causes an argument later. Fixing the number of rounds puts both sides at ease.

If You Have to Narrow the Budget, Where Do You Cut?

What you cut isn't the number of shots but the variety of setups. Coming down to one location is the quickest route. Moving from a cast to product-led storytelling, and narrowing the number of channels, also work. Cutting the preparation, on the other hand, backfires. The most expensive way to bring the price of a commercial film down is to skip the preparation and decide on set.

When you make the decision to cut, write down what you're losing too. Coming down to one location protects the budget but narrows the story; product-led storytelling is cheap but weakens the emotional connection.

Planning the visual production from one place is another way to save. It's possible to take product photographs on the same shooting day; we covered the approach in the product photographs piece. We set out how to choose the team in the choosing an agency piece.

Do the cutting round together with the agency. Deleting line items on your own usually deletes the wrong one. The team doing the work knows better which cut weakens the story and where.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions: the price of a commercial film

Why isn't the price of a commercial film given as a clear list?

Because jobs that look nothing alike sit under the same heading. A promotional film shot in one location, with no cast, for a single channel doesn't carry the same line items as a job shot across three locations, with a cast, delivered to five channels including television. Rather than giving a list, we explain the line items; once your scope is clear, the figure becomes clear too. Whoever hands you a ready-made list has usually narrowed the scope on your behalf. And the difference usually surfaces after the contract is signed.

Which is the biggest line item?

Usually the shooting day and the number of setups.

Why is the performer's usage period priced separately?

Because you're renting a performer's face for a defined period and defined channels. When the period runs out, or when you add a new channel, a fresh agreement is needed.

Would it be cheaper to shoot in our own office?

It largely removes the location line item. But if the sound insulation and lighting conditions aren't suitable, you may give back on equipment what you saved.

Is a stock library enough for the music?

It's enough for most jobs. Read which channels and which period the licence covers before you buy.

How many channel versions should the quote include?

Every channel you'll publish on should be written out separately. Each ratio added later means another edit round.

Why is a cheap quote cheap?

Usually because it has read the scope narrowly. A quote that doesn't account for the post-production line items, the channel versions and the performers' rights looks cheap at the start and turns out expensive at the end.

Should I say my budget up front?

If you do, we design the scope around it.
