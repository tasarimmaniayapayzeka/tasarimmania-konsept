What Does Corporate Graphic Design Cover?

A brand's face takes shape afresh in every visual you come across. Corporate graphic design is the umbrella gathering all of those visuals under a single language. The number of businesses going digital in Türkiye is rising fast. The Ministry of Trade's Outlook for E-Commerce in Türkiye 2025 report puts the number of businesses trading online at 634,611. For SMEs wanting to stand apart by their visual language in that crowding shop window, getting to know the types of design is the first step. In this article we set out what you will see, from the logo to the packaging and from the catalogue to the social media image. We aren't going into software or stages of production.

What Is Corporate Graphic Design, and Which Outputs Does It Cover?

Corporate graphic design is the design discipline producing a brand's logo, identity set, print work and digital visuals. All of those outputs carry a shared visual language. Its scope isn't limited to a single logo. It contains a wide range of outputs running from the business card to the packaging and from the catalogue to the web banner. The aim is for the brand to appear with the same character at every touchpoint.

A visual identity is the body of rules defining a brand through colour, typeface and form. The industry also calls that same whole the brand identity. On the SME side, the value of that whole shows up on the shelf and on the screen. A customer remembers a brand with a consistent appearance more easily. The corporate leg of graphic design is precisely the family of outputs producing that consistency.

Why Is the Range of Visual Outputs So Wide?

Your customer meets the brand on different surfaces: a phone screen, a shop shelf, a trade fair stand, an invoice envelope. Each surface has its own size and light; each asks for its own output. That wide range of outputs isn't a luxury but the natural result of having touchpoints.

What Will You Find in the Logo and Identity Set?

The logo is the smallest but most visible output at the centre of the identity set. The business card, the letterhead, the email signature and the envelope surround it. Your brand then appears consistently, with the same face, everywhere. Planning that whole under a single roof gives a more consistent result than producing it piece by piece.

The identity set is the family of concrete pieces running from your firm's official correspondence to the field team's staff badge. What matters is less the number of pieces than the kinship between them. The colour and the typeface stay shared across all of them. A small SME example makes it clear. A food wholesaler sends a proposal with an email signature in the morning. At midday they hand over a business card, and in the evening they arrive at the customer's door in a delivery vehicle. If those three touchpoints carry the same colours, the customer perceives a single brand. If the set is scattered, each contact leaves the impression of a different firm.

The Logo: The Brand's Smallest Stage

The emblem and the logotype are two members of the same family: the emblem carries the symbol, the logotype the words. A good logo shrinks on a business card, grows on a sign and stays legible at both sizes. On delivery you see the colour, single-colour and negative-ground versions together.

Which Pieces Make Up the Identity Set?

A typical set contains a business card, letterhead, envelope, folder, email signature and presentation template. For teams working in the field, a staff badge and vehicle livery join the range too.

What Will You Find in the Brand Guidelines?

Brand guidelines are the reference document gathering the rules for using the logo, the colour codes and the typefaces into a single file. For SMEs, even a short version largely protects consistency.

In Print: The Range from Packaging to Catalogue

On the print side, packaging, labels, catalogues, brochures and trade fair materials are the most tangible outputs of corporate graphic design. Colours that look vivid on screen can change on paper; print work therefore asks for its own colour standard. Physical stages such as the shelf and the stand enter that list too, each expecting production at its own size.

The advantage of a physical output is that it lasts. The image on a screen disappears; a catalogue sitting on a desk passes from hand to hand for days. On colour, the screen works with RGB light while the press works with CMYK ink. Because of that difference, an orange that looks lively on screen can come out flat on paper. The choice of paper changes the result too. Matt coated gives a calm, corporate feeling. Gloss coated brings colours forward, and kraft paper carries a natural character. Asking for a colour proof before printing solves at the desk the surprise you would otherwise have on the shelf.

How Does Packaging Design Look on the Shelf?

Packaging design is the visual surface introducing a product on the shelf within seconds. The industry also knows this type as pack design. The box, the label, the bag and the packing tape are all parts of the same family. Which box the eye goes to first among competing products determines the success of that output.

What Should You Expect from a Catalogue and a Brochure?

A catalogue is the multi-page output describing your product family through page layout. A brochure — the short promotional booklet — is its version focused on a single subject. On both, the quality of the photography and the space on the page carry the look. In trade fair periods those two become your sales team's shop window on the table.

Which Types of Design Will You See on Screen?

On the digital side, social media visuals, web banners, newsletter templates and advertising creatives form the backbone of the everyday appearance. A brand's face on screen now matters as much as a shop window. Every channel asks for its own dimensions; you cannot reach everywhere with a single visual, and each format is rebuilt at its own ratio.

TurkStat— TurkStat is Türkiye's statistical institute — and its latest research shows household internet use rising steadily over the years. The screen may be where many of your customers meet your brand for the first time. It is sensible to plan social media designs together with the social media management service. Site images, meanwhile, are best thought through together with the web design and development infrastructure; that protects the unity of the look.

What Is There in Social Media Visuals?

Post templates, story designs, cover images and profile elements are the members of this family. The logic of a template makes it clear at first glance that every post belongs to your brand. In design, a corporate graphic language makes recognition possible even for an eye scrolling quickly past.

Advertising Creatives and Banner Sets

We prepare digital advertising visuals as a set, with square, vertical and landscape versions. All of them are parts of a single campaign. Running the campaign design jointly with the digital marketing team strengthens the link between the visual and the targeting.

Which Visual Output Answers Which Need?

Your order of need takes shape according to the stage your brand is at. At a new founding the identity comes forward, at a product launch the packaging, and in a period of growth the digital set. Going in the wrong order splits the budget and breaks the appearance into pieces. The sequence below shows the order of need we meet most often at SMEs, through four typical scenarios.

  1. A newly founded business: starts with the logo, the identity set and the brand guidelines.
  2. A brand whose product is going onto the shelf: puts packaging and label design first.
  3. A firm selling through dealers or B2B: turns to the catalogue and the promotional booklet.
  4. A business growing in the digital channel: invests in social media templates and an advertising set.
Designing the corporate graphic design investment in that order lets each output sit on top of the one before it.

How Is the Look Protected When Needs Are Mixed?

If you need several outputs at once, your anchor is the brand guidelines. With the guidelines ready, one team can produce the packaging and another the social media. The result still looks as though it comes from the same family. The graphic face of corporate communication stays standing through that shared reference.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions: corporate graphic design

Does corporate graphic design just mean working on a logo?

No; the logo is only the central piece of the range. Corporate graphic design covers the identity set, the packaging, the labels, the catalogue and the brochure. Social media templates, web banners and the brand guidelines fall within that scope too. A business doesn't need all of those outputs at once. The order of priority changes with the stage the brand is at. What matters is that every piece produced looks as though it belongs to the same visual family. Planning the range as a whole also makes it easier for the pieces added later to be born in keeping with it.

What is the difference between a logo and an emblem?

The emblem is the symbol representing the brand; the logotype is the distinctive setting of the brand name. The two used together are what everyday language calls the logo.

Which type of design is the priority for a small business?

At newly founded businesses the priority is usually the logo, a basic identity set and a short set of brand guidelines. Once those three are ready, packaging, a catalogue or a social media set are easily added on top of the same language.

Is packaging design necessary for brands selling online too?

Yes; in e-commerce the delivery box and the product photograph take the place of the shelf. The experience of opening the box, the inner packing and the labels may be the only moment the brand makes physical contact with the customer.

Can I have social media templates made without brand guidelines?

Technically yes; in practice it is risky. Without guidelines, every new designer picks the colours and the typeface on their own interpretation. Over time your feed starts to look as though it belongs to different brands. Even a short set of guidelines prevents that drift.

Is a digital PDF enough instead of a catalogue?

It depends on your audience. If you work mainly through trade fairs and face-to-face selling, a printed catalogue leaves a lasting mark on the table. If you send proposals by email, the digital version of the same design will do. Deriving both formats from a single design is the most economical route.

How many pieces make up an identity set?

There is no standard number; the pieces are settled by the business's touchpoints. A firm working from a desk makes do with a business card, letterhead and an email signature. A firm with a field team adds vehicle livery and staff badges.

Can we keep our existing logo and have the other outputs renewed?

Yes; taking your existing logo as the base, the identity set, the packaging and the digital templates can all be produced in its language.
