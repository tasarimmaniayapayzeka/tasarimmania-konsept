Email Marketing Strategy: Is There a Right Time to Send?

Monday morning at 09:00, or Thursday evening at 21:00? That is the question we hear most often from SME managers. Rather than hunting for a single magic hour, we recommend building an email marketing strategy grounded in measurement; because the right moment varies with the sector, the audience and the kind of message. In this article we explain how to settle the send time, the sending frequency and the timing of automation flows using data. Planning the advertising budget and writing ad copy are subjects for separate articles; our focus here is timing alone. At the end of the article you'll also find a test arrangement you can apply to your own list straight away.

What Is an Email Marketing Strategy, and Why Is Timing at Its Centre?

An email marketing strategy is the plan defining whom you will send what content to, when and how often. Timing is the leg of that plan most often neglected. The same content sent at the wrong hour is lost at the bottom of the inbox. A message that arrives at the right moment gets read, gets clicked and turns into a sale. You have to build your plan across the hour, day and lifecycle layers together.

The number of businesses moving into a digital shop window in Türkiye is rising fast. The ETBİS 2025 report— ETBİS is Türkiye's central e-commerce information system — shows the number of businesses trading online reaching 634,611. Being visible in a crowding inbox no longer ends with good content. You also have to knock at the right moment. In our digital marketing service we therefore run the email channel on a timing calendar of its own.

The Three Layers of Timing: Hour, Day and Lifecycle

The hour layer defines which part of the day you send in. The day layer sets the weekly rhythm. The lifecycle layer shows which stage the recipient is at. A new subscriber and a buyer who has been silent for months don't deserve the same calendar. A sound plan tests those three layers separately. It writes a different rule for each.

Is There a Single Send Time That Suits Everyone?

No; there is no single ideal send time that works across every sector. The right hour comes out of your own list's data. General research points to the weekday morning and midday range. Those findings are only a starting hypothesis. A simple two-week test of times produces a far more accurate compass for an email marketing strategy than any general table.

Habits of internet use affect the hour directly. TurkStat's latest research — TurkStat is Türkiye's statistical institute — sets out how household internet use has spread. As reading on mobile grows, evening slots gain value for B2C brands. On the email leg of your strategy you have to test tendencies of that kind against your own data.

Rhythms That Vary by Audience

B2B buyers are more active within working hours, mostly in the Tuesday-to-Thursday range. B2C subscribers, on the other hand, are more inclined to open a newsletter in the evening and at weekends. The rhythms of retail, education and services diverge from one another. Rather than leaning on a sector average, watch your own segment.

How Do You Set Up Your Own Time Test?

You can set the test up in four steps:
  1. Split your list at random into two equal groups.
  2. Send the same newsletter to the two groups at two different times.
  3. Compare the open and click rates 48 hours later.
  4. Race the winning time against a new alternative in the next round.
At the end of four rounds you have a map of times specific to you. That map is far more valuable than general advice.

How Should the Sending Frequency Be Set?

The sending frequency should be often enough for the subscriber to remember the brand and sparse enough that they don't tire of it. For SMEs, 2–4 newsletters a month is a balanced start. Holding the frequency steady is a safer route than occasional bursts of heavy campaigning. If your unsubscribe rate is rising, your tempo is more than your audience can carry.

When we build an email marketing strategy we watch three signals on the frequency side. They are the unsubscribe rate, the complaint rate and how engagement moves over time. Reading the three together sets out the health of the list clearly. None of them is enough on its own.

Frequency Signals: What Should You Watch?

Three signals offer a practical compass. If your unsubscribe rate climbs noticeably from send to send, reduce the frequency. If the open rate falls on three sends in a row, change the hour and the mix of content. If spam complaints start trending upwards, review both your tempo and the source of your list. Tracking those signals in the same report every month lets you catch an adverse trend early.

Rhythm Signals for Staying Out of the Spam Folder

Mail services reward regular sender behaviour. A sudden wave of sending after a long silence damages the sender reputation. That wave pushes messages into the promotions or spam tab. A steady frequency is therefore as important as the technical infrastructure.

How Do You Design Timing in Automation Flows?

An automation flow is the part of an email marketing strategy that is triggered by a subscriber's behaviour and moves forward on predefined waiting periods. The welcome message should go out minutes after sign-up. The basket reminder comes into play within 1–24 hours, and the re-engagement message within a window of 60–90 days of silence. In automation, timing depends less on the clock than on the trigger.

A campaign calendar runs at the hour you set. An automatic flow runs at the subscriber's hour. A strong email marketing strategy designs the two arrangements to complete one another.

Waiting Periods in the Welcome Series

The first message should go out immediately after sign-up. Interest is at its peak at that moment. Planning the second message for 2–3 days later and the third for a week on completes the series without wearing it out. Keeping each step focused on a single subject makes the recipient's decision easier.

Abandoned Basket and Re-engagement

On a basket reminder, sending the first message within 1–4 hours and the second the next day is a common and balanced arrangement. The e-commerce platform we build for brands lets us feed these flows directly from site data. The re-engagement flow reaches a buyer who has been silent for 60–90 days with a reminder. If no reply comes, cleaning the list protects the sender reputation.

Which Data Should You Measure Timing Decisions With?

Measure timing decisions together with the open rate, the click rate, unsubscribes and conversion data. A single metric on its own misleads. The open rate offers a quick indicator for time tests. To see the effect on revenue, track clicks and conversions. A good email marketing strategy ties every timing decision to those metrics.

On how widespread mobile access is, the data published by BTK— BTK is Türkiye's information and communication technologies authority — shows reading behaviour spread across devices. Examining the reports with a device breakdown is therefore important. For reading that breakdown we recommend a simple arrangement. Split your send report into two columns, desktop and mobile. On every time test, note the difference in opens and clicks between the two columns separately. On a mobile-led list, put the evening slots into the test plan; on a desktop-led list, working hours. That breakdown protects you from false generalisations when you interpret timing reports. We combine the timing findings with channel data in our performance marketing reports, and we align the topic plan with our content strategy team.

Open Rate, or Clicks?

The open rate is the metric showing the percentage of recipients who viewed the email. Because of privacy measures it is no longer taken as definitive on its own. The click rate measures behaviour, conversion measures revenue. Teams that read the open rate and the clicks together in time tests take far more accurate decisions.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions: email marketing strategy

Which is the best day to send an email?

There is no universal 'best day'. On B2B lists the Tuesday-to-Thursday range and on B2C lists the evening and weekend slots usually perform better; the firm answer comes from a two-week test on your own list.

When should the welcome email be sent?

It should be sent immediately after sign-up; that is the moment the subscriber's interest is highest.

How many newsletters a month is right?

For SMEs, 2–4 newsletters a month is a balanced start. If your unsubscribe rate isn't rising and engagement is holding, you can raise the frequency gradually.

How many hours later should the abandoned basket email be sent?

In common practice the first reminder goes out within 1–4 hours and the second message the next day. A third message raises the risk of complaints on most lists.

If my open rate is low, what should I change first?

We recommend testing three things in order: the send time first, then the subject line, and the frequency last. The time test is the one that gives results fastest; split the list in two, send the same content at two different times, and you can measure it within 48 hours. On a subject line test, change only one variable at a time. The effect of a change in frequency becomes clear over several weeks of tracking; that is why you need a patient measurement schedule.

Is sending email at night harmful?

What does harm isn't the hour but being out of the ordinary. If your audience is used to reading at night you won't have a problem; if you are trying it for the first time, test it on a small segment.

What is the difference between an automation flow and a campaign send?

A campaign is a single send going to the whole list on a date you choose. An automation flow is an automatic sequence triggered by each subscriber's own behaviour, with waiting periods defined in advance; that is why everyone receives the same message at a different time.
