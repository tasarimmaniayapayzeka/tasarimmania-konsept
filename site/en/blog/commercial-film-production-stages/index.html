What Stages Does a Commercial Film Shoot Go Through?

The work doesn't begin when the camera rolls; by then it should already be done. Decisions made on set cost many times more than decisions made at the desk. In this piece we go through the five stages in order: pre-production, the shoot, the edit, colour and sound, delivery. We give the typical length of each stage too. We're not talking about price; there's only sequence and timing here.

What Stages Does a Commercial Film Shoot Go Through?

There are five stages: pre-production, the shoot, the edit, colour and sound, delivery. What sets the timeline isn't the number of stages but how tightly you keep the pre-production. A commercial film shoot takes a day, but the work that prepares that day takes weeks. Every decision made after the set is built is expensive.

Thinking of the stages as a straight line misleads. The search for music starts in pre-production and carries on through the edit. The delivery formats, meanwhile, should be known while the script is being written; a channel learned about later throws the framing decisions away.

The order below is the one we run.

  1. Pre-production — script, storyboard, location, cast, shot list.
  2. The shoot — building the set, getting the shots, safety takes.
  3. The edit — rough cut, revision rounds, fine cut.
  4. Colour and sound — colour grade, voiceover, music, mix.
  5. Delivery — ratio, duration and subtitle versions per channel.

How Long Does It Take in Total?

On a narrow job, four to six weeks is a reasonable target. Once a cast, several locations and special effects come in, it widens towards two months. How much of the commercial gets filmed on the shooting day sets that timeline directly.

What Does the Team Settle in Pre-Production?

This stage puts everything on paper, from the script to the shot list. The location, the cast, the lighting plan and the shooting order are all settled here. A storyboard comes out of it too, so the frame can be argued about at the desk rather than on set. Going into a commercial film shoot unprepared is the most expensive way to save money. This stage takes two to four weeks.

Pre-production work looks dull and is the first line item to be cut. Yet an hour on set is worth a day at the desk. Decisions taken while the crew, the cast and the location are all waiting at once come out both fast and bad.

The storyboard is the most visible output of this stage; we set out what it does in the storyboard piece. It also helps to think about it alongside the message structure in the writing ad copy piece; the picture and the copy settle at the same time. The channel rules have to be known before the shoot; RTÜK's broadcasting principles set limits on duration and content.

Why Does the Shot List Matter So Much?

Because a set runs on the clock. The list shows which shot is filmed in which order, and it groups the lighting setups. A day without a list goes on building the same setup three times.

How Does the Shooting Day Run?

The day runs to the shot list, not to the order of the story. We take the shots that happen in the same place one after another, and then the lighting setup changes. On jobs with a cast, rehearsals finish in the morning. A commercial film shoot usually fits into a single day, but the day is never shorter than ten hours. Squeezing in too many shots drops the quality by the end of the day.

The morning starts with the build, and the first shot usually lands towards midday. In the afternoon the pace picks up; the crew speeds up as it gets to know the setup. The last two hours are the most productive, which is why we don't put the difficult shots there.

Taking safety shots is part of the day too. A slightly wider and a slightly tighter version of the same shot make life easier in the edit. That few minutes of work saves you from going back for a reshoot.

The weather and the light shape the day as well. If you're working outdoors, the golden hours are short and expensive; we put those shots first. Deciding before the shoot which light the advertising idea will live in wins hours back during the day.

Why Is a Decision on Set Expensive?

Because at that moment everyone is waiting. The crew, the equipment and the location all spend the same hour. A decision that takes five minutes at the desk costs fifty times more on set.

How Long Do the Edit and Colour Stages Take?

The edit takes one to two weeks, revision rounds included. Colour and sound are a separate stage and usually take a few days. The music licence can drag on unexpectedly; we start that early. When a commercial film shoot ends, the work doesn't; the second half happens at the desk. Fixing the number of revisions in the contract puts both sides at ease.

The first delivery is the rough cut and comes without music. Its purpose is to argue about the rhythm, not the beauty. Structural comments given at this stage are easy to apply; after the fine cut, the same comment costs far more.

The colour grade sets the brand's tone. The same footage gives a completely different feeling in a warm or a cool grade. On the sound side, we work the music, the voiceover and the effects mix separately.

Use a timecode when you give comments. “That scene is long” isn't enough. “Take two seconds off the shot at 00:12” shortens a round on its own. Scattered comments force the editor to guess, and a guess produces another round.

How Many Revision Rounds Are Normal?

Two rounds is normal. If a third round is going round, the problem isn't in the edit but in a decision left open in pre-production. At that point we take a step back and talk again about what we're trying to say.

What Do We Check Before Delivery?

The output isn't a single file. For every channel it will be published on, we produce a separate aspect ratio, a separate duration and separate subtitles. On channels watched without sound, subtitles are mandatory, not optional. The footage that comes out of a commercial film shoot is the same, but the delivery pack changes with the channel. Talking that pack through at the start prevents a re-edit later.

Vertical, square and horizontal ratios each want different framing. When a shot filmed horizontally is cropped to vertical, the product usually ends up outside the frame. That's why we need to know during the shoot which ratios will be required.

Duration changes with the channel too. The six-second and thirty-second versions of the same story are edited separately. We covered choosing the channel in the social media advertising piece.

File naming is a small but critical detail as well. If you write the channel, the ratio and the duration into the name, nobody uploads the wrong file on publication day. That habit removes the most common mistake between an agency and a media team.

FAQs

Frequently asked questions: the stages of a commercial film shoot

How long does a commercial film shoot take in total?

It varies with scope, but a range can be given. On a job in one location, with no cast and no effects, four to six weeks is a reasonable target. Once casting, several locations, special effects or a foreign-language voiceover come in, it widens towards two months. What sets that range isn't the number of shooting days but how many decisions are left open in pre-production. Projects that close their decisions early always finish sooner.

How many days does the shoot take?

One day is enough on most jobs.

Can you go into a shoot without a storyboard?

You can, but we wouldn't advise it. A storyboard lets you argue about the frame at the desk rather than on set; without one, the same argument happens while the crew waits.

How much does using a cast add to the schedule?

It usually adds a week for casting and rehearsal. The contract and usage-period discussions are part of that time too.

When should we get the music licence?

As early as possible. The licensing process can drag on unpredictably and hold up the edit.

How many different versions are delivered?

It depends on how many channels you'll publish on. As well as the horizontal, vertical and square ratios, different durations are edited separately too.

Can a new shot be added after filming?

Only with a reshoot. That's why taking safety shots on the shooting day is cheap insurance against needs that come up later.

Are subtitles essential?

On channels watched without sound, they are.
